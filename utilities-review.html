04

The finding that matters

Across all 92 days with electricity readings, occupancy barely explains consumption at all. The correlation between room nights and daily kWh is 0.22 — statistically, occupancy accounts for about 5% of what we burn.

Fit a line through the data and it comes out at roughly 1,890 kWh a day fixed, plus about 2 kWh for each additional room night. Around 75% of the electricity bill is being spent whether guests are in the building or not.

That is the whole opportunity in one sentence. Chasing guest behaviour — towel cards, key-tag switches, low-flow heads — is working on the 25%. The plant that runs regardless of occupancy is where the money is: hot water generation, pool plant, kitchen refrigeration, external and back-of-house lighting, and anything left on a timer that no longer matches how the building is used.

July 2026, day by day

Two weeks at 3,446 kWh a day, then from 15 July it drops to 2,166 and stays there — a 37% fall. Occupancy does not explain it. The 15th and 16th were the two busiest nights of the month, 108 and 109 rooms, and they used less electricity than the 18th, when we had seven rooms occupied.

Either something with a ~1,200 kWh/day load came off on 15 July, or the meter changed what it was reading. Nobody wrote a note against either day. This is the single most valuable question in the file and I can't answer it from the data.

If it's a genuine plant change it is worth roughly R1.0 million a year at R2.50/kWh and we need to know exactly what did it so it doesn't quietly get reversed. If it's a metering change, then the first-half figures are inflated and every intensity number for July is wrong. Same number, two completely opposite conclusions, and the only thing separating them is a note in a column we leave blank.

For context: at March 2026 and April 2025 levels the property ran at about 1,920 kWh a day. On that basis the 1–14 July period was 19,881 kWh above expectation — roughly R50,000 in two weeks.

One more tell

Eight of the 92 daily electricity readings are exact duplicates of another day, to one decimal place. Meters do not repeat themselves to 0.1 kWh. Those readings were carried forward, not read.

05

What I'm changing

The workbook doesn't need replacing. It needs the capture discipline the design already assumes, and about a day of repair work on the formulas.

This week

Stop the bleeding

  1. Fix the four critical water errorsEnter the opening meter reading in the row above day one on every tab, replace the three hard-typed water totals with formulas, and re-derive the June 2025 rollover from the raw readings. Until this is done every water number we report is fiction.
  2. Correct the 19 July room-night entry and the hard-typed July totalBoth are single-cell fixes. Both currently make our energy performance look better than it is.
  3. Find out what happened on 15 JulyCross-check the WhatsApp log, the job cards and the hot water plant work for that date. Write the answer into the Notes column against 15 July 2026 so it survives.
  4. Rename the three mislabelled tabsApril/May/June 2026 become April/May/June 2025, and open fresh tabs for the current year.
This month

Make capture non-optional

  1. Readings move onto the night shift checklistElectricity, water and generator hours, same time every night, by the person already walking the plant rooms. It is a four-minute job. The five-month blackout happened because it belonged to nobody.
  2. One entry convention, written at the top of every tabColumn M is the cumulative meter reading, always. Consumption is calculated, never typed. The three-way mix in the current file is why nothing reconciles.
  3. Validation on the input cellsRoom nights capped at the room count, a warning if today's meter reading is lower than yesterday's, and conditional formatting on any intensity outside 0–1,000. A negative water figure should never be able to reach the Profile tab.
  4. Make the Notes column compulsory on any day that moves more than 25%The 15 July question would already be answered if this rule had existed.
  5. Settle the recycling target35% or 25%. Pick one, delete the other, and start weighing landfill so the percentage means something.
This quarter

Go after the baseload

  1. Overnight load auditTwo nights at low occupancy, walking the property between 02:00 and 04:00 with a clamp meter, logging what is drawing power. That gives us the fixed 1,890 kWh a day broken down by system instead of as one number.
  2. Sub-meter the four biggest loadsHot water plant, pool plant, kitchen and laundry. One number for the whole property tells us we have a problem; it never tells us where. Sub-meters are the cheapest diagnostic we can buy.
  3. Fill in the cost rowsRows 39–43 of the Profile tab are empty, so total utilities reads R0. Kilowatt-hours don't get anyone's attention upstairs. Rands do — and at R2.50/kWh we're spending roughly R142,000 a month on the fixed load alone.
  4. Re-baseline once we have six clean monthsThe current 2025 baseline is built partly on room-night figures pasted into the water row. Any saving measured against it is meaningless. I'd rather set an honest baseline late than report a saving I can't defend.
