Hospitality stories

A hotel is a promise delivered through a building, and the promise is tested in small moments: a cold shower, a rattling fan, a review that says the room did not work. These are some of the moments that shaped how I run a department.

01
The Moorings · May 2026

Taking over with nothing

What happened

I took the department over with no handover. No jobcard history, no asset register, no preventive maintenance schedule, no contractor file, no record of what had been promised to whom.

What I did

Recovered 194 historical job entries out of team communications to build a room history where none existed, then built the asset register, PM schedule, contractor file and compliance framework behind it. The whole management framework stood within four months.

What it taught me

If it is not written down, it did not happen. The first work in any building is to find out what is installed and record what it is doing.

02
The Moorings · Hot water plant

The shower that took three minutes

What happened

Guests were waiting two to three minutes for hot water at the outlet. The main building had about 1,500 litres of storage against an estimated 7,500-litre demand, and eight boiler element failures in twelve months.

What I did

Built the case from first principles and escalated the Legionella exposure as a compliance and trading risk, not a comfort complaint. Moved the project off a R926,085 boiler quote onto heat pumps and accumulators: the capacity needed, roughly R214,000 less on the quoted figures, with two independent heat pump modules for redundancy. Supervised the install around a full, trading hotel.

What it taught me

A capital motivation should be arithmetic a financial director can check. And a lifecycle decision, like coil protection against salt air, belongs at specification stage, not after the first failure.

03
Hyatt House Sandton

Five who said no

What happened

The guest-satisfaction target was 90% or better. In one review cycle there were 42 reviews and 5 'working order: no' responses. Three of those five were preventable.

What I did

Rebuilt the preventive maintenance schedules across electrical, HVAC, plumbing and critical systems. Maintenance efficiency rose 35%, and guest satisfaction went from 70% to a 90% average within the first quarter.

What it taught me

Most of what a guest notices is something that could have been caught earlier. Preventable failures are the argument for planned maintenance.

A Michelin Key, and a delegation

What happened

I directed the pre-opening and launch of a 5-star hotel across facilities, engineering, security and health and safety. The property earned Michelin Key recognition within four months of opening.

Recognition of that kind is won in the details a guest never has to notice.

06
The Moorings · Hot water

A boiler at the end of its life

What happened

The main hot water storage vessel was found leaking, not from any pipe or fitting but from the body of the tank itself, with visible corrosion. The rate of loss kept increasing. The risk was a full failure, and rooms closing for lack of hot water.

What I did

Documented the condition and the failure mode in a written report, kept a limited hot water supply going by repairing the existing heat pump system, and recommended replacement rather than a repair that could not hold. That report is the start of the case for the new heat pump and accumulator plant.

What it taught me

Test capacity against the worst day, not the average day, and write the failure down while it is still happening.

07
The Moorings · Water network

The pressure that came back

What happened

The pressure-reducing valve failed and pressure swung. It was replaced, the instability continued, and a second replacement finally restored normal pressure. Then the network failed: three pipe ruptures at once, hidden leaks in wall cavities across the rooms, and the hot water vessel breached through its shell.

What I did

Patched the active leaks, monitored the meters and pressures, tracked moisture room by room, and wrote it up: copper pipework corroded inside and out, one 7 m section carrying four repair couplings, and a network that had only survived at low pressure. The recommendation was a new ring main, with the pipe infrastructure report quantifying the exposure.

What it taught me

Restoring normal pressure to an old system is itself a risk event. Know the condition before you fix the fault, because the fix can expose what the fault was hiding.

The Moorings

The meter that kept moving

What happened

Consumption looked high. The meter read 2,467,312 at the start and 2,468,421 about five and a half hours later.

What I did

Used the readings to size the problem with numbers instead of opinions, and fed it into the utilities workbook, rebuilt with regression, CUSUM and municipal bill reconciliation to expose consumption drift and billing errors.

What it taught me

Measure, then argue. The meter does not have an opinion.

10
The Moorings · 27 August 2026

Fifteen minutes

What happened

The property lost municipal power and went onto generator. When power came back to the plant room, the booster pumps restarted automatically and a 50 mm copper cold water inlet separated at a joint, discharging into the plant room. It was isolated at 09:15, fifteen minutes later. No one was hurt.

What I did

Made the plant room safe, checked the electrics for water before re-energising anything, and had it repaired the same day. Then I wrote the incident report, and in it said what the repair does not do. The cause was a pressure surge on pump restart. The new brackets fix the symptom, not the cause, so I listed the staggered restart, the arrestor, the check valves and the support checks that remain open.

What it taught me

Write down what the repair does not fix. Disclose it in full, so nothing material is withheld, and so the next surge finds the real fix already on the list.

11
The Moorings · Friday 8 May 2026

Feedback is the breakfast of champions

What happened

After a run of storm incidents I sat the team down for thirty minutes on a Friday afternoon and asked what was working and what was not. They said prioritisation had improved, that fewer outside contractor callouts made them feel trusted, and that they felt involved rather than just watching. They singled out the coordinated flood response at the Sunset Basement. They also raised PPE availability, the boilers, the pipes and exposed wiring in the gardens, and asked for training in electrical safety, leak detection and boiler operation.

What I did

Wrote the meeting up with every issue turned into an action: an owner, a deadline and a status. PPE stock review, the boiler investigation, an urgent inspection of the garden wiring and a training schedule all went on the list. I run the same format weekly, alongside daily planning meetings and war rooms for big projects and urgent work.

What it taught me

A team will tell you exactly what it needs if you ask in a format that ends in an action. And praise belongs at the top of the agenda: people repeat what gets recognised.

Every one of these ends the same way: write it down and fix the cause.
